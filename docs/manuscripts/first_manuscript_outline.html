Bayesian Functional Validation of Simulated Human Skin Reflectance Spectra Using the International Skin Spectra Archive

ISSA reflectance, derivative shape analysis, BVCM modeling, and spectrum validation

Introduction

Motivation

  • Human skin reflectance spectra are used in dermatology, imaging, cosmetics, computer graphics, and fairness-sensitive computer vision applications.
  • Simulated spectra can expand coverage of skin conditions and acquisition settings, but their scientific value depends on validation against real measurements.
  • Validation should account for full-spectrum behavior, not only colorimetric summaries or pointwise error metrics.

Gap

  • Existing validation often emphasizes visual agreement, broadband color summaries, or parameter-level plausibility.
  • Skin reflectance spectra contain structured wavelength-dependent variation, including shape features associated with melanin, hemoglobin absorption, and scattering.
  • A statistical validation framework should evaluate both spectral level and spectral shape under uncertainty.

Objective

Use ISSA as an empirical reference dataset to build and demonstrate a BVCM-based framework for validating simulated spectra.

Expected Contributions

  • Descriptive baseline for ISSA reflectance spectra.
  • Derivative-based representation of spectral shape.
  • BVCM estimation strategy for spectral reflectance.
  • Validation scores and diagnostics for simulated spectra.

Derivative-Based Shape Analysis

Rationale

  • Reflectance level and spectral shape capture different aspects of skin appearance.
  • First derivatives highlight slope and monotonicity patterns.
  • Second derivatives highlight curvature and absorption-related local structure.
  • Derivative features can make simulation discrepancies visible even when spectra have similar overall brightness.

Method

  • Smooth reflectance spectra before numerical differentiation.
  • Compute first derivative with respect to wavelength.
  • Compute second derivative with respect to wavelength.
  • Define shape summaries such as:
    • integrated absolute first derivative,
    • wavelength-specific slope extrema,
    • curvature extrema,
    • derivative-based distances between spectra,
    • region-specific derivative summaries.

Descriptive Shape Results

  • Characterize derivative distributions across wavelengths.
  • Identify dominant slope and curvature regions.
  • Compare derivative features across skin tone or metadata groups.
  • Evaluate whether simulated spectra reproduce ISSA derivative patterns.

BVCM Model: Method and Estimation

Modeling Goal

Estimate wavelength-varying associations between reflectance and covariates while preserving uncertainty in the functional response.

Model Specification

Let (Y_i()) denote reflectance for observation (i) at wavelength (). A working BVCM can be written as:

[ Y_i() = 0() + {p=1}^{P} x_{ip}_p() + _i(), ]

where (_p()) are wavelength-varying coefficient functions and (_i()) captures residual spectral variation.

Covariates

  • Candidate ISSA covariates:
    • colorimetric summaries,
    • demographic variables,
    • anatomical site,
    • measurement or acquisition descriptors.
  • Candidate simulation covariates:
    • melanin-related parameters,
    • hemoglobin or erythema-related parameters,
    • scattering parameters,
    • thickness or layer parameters.

Prior Structure

  • Use smoothness-inducing priors for coefficient functions.
  • Account for wavelength-wise correlation in residuals when feasible.
  • Include hierarchical terms for repeated observations or subject-level clustering if supported by data structure.

Estimation

  • Estimate posterior distributions for coefficient functions.
  • Report convergence diagnostics and posterior predictive checks.
  • Compare alternative model forms:
    • intercept-only empirical reference model,
    • covariate-adjusted BVCM,
    • derivative-informed or derivative-validated extensions.

BVCM-Based Spectrum Validation Framework

Validation Objective

Assess whether a simulated spectrum is compatible with the empirical spectral distribution represented by the fitted BVCM.

Validation Inputs

  • Fitted BVCM from ISSA spectra.
  • Simulated spectra generated from the SkinSim pipeline.
  • Covariate or parameter information needed to condition the validation model.

Validation Targets

  • Pointwise validity:
    • wavelength-specific posterior predictive intervals,
    • local exceedance probabilities,
    • regions of systematic over- or under-reflectance.
  • Conditional validity:
    • compatibility given observed or simulated covariates,
    • subgroup-specific validation when metadata are available.
  • Whole-spectrum validity:
    • posterior predictive distance,
    • integrated residual summaries,
    • derivative-based shape discrepancy.

Proposed Validation Metrics

  • Pointwise coverage indicator across wavelength.
  • Integrated absolute standardized residual.
  • Maximum absolute standardized residual.
  • Derivative discrepancy score.
  • Posterior predictive p-value or tail probability.
  • Composite validity score combining level and shape diagnostics.

Application to Simulated Spectra

  • Apply validation framework to the existing simulated spectrum set.
  • Compare validation results across simulation parameter regimes.
  • Identify spectra that pass level-based checks but fail shape-based checks.
  • Identify wavelength regions where simulations deviate most from ISSA.

Discussion

Main Findings

  • Summarize empirical ISSA spectral patterns.
  • Interpret derivative-based shape features.
  • Discuss wavelength-varying effects from the BVCM.
  • Explain what the validation framework reveals about simulated spectra.

Implications

  • Statistical validation can improve simulation pipelines by identifying wavelength regions and shape features needing refinement.
  • BVCM-based validation offers uncertainty-aware diagnostics rather than binary acceptance rules.
  • The framework can support future simulation studies, sensor design, and fairness-aware skin appearance modeling.

Limitations

  • ISSA measurement conditions and metadata may limit generalizability.
  • BVCM assumptions about smoothness, covariance, and covariate availability require sensitivity analysis.
  • Simulated spectra may have parameters that do not map directly to ISSA metadata.
  • Derivative estimates depend on smoothing and wavelength resolution.

Future Work

  • Extend validation to additional real spectral datasets.
  • Incorporate hierarchical subject-level effects more fully.
  • Model derivative spectra jointly with reflectance spectra.
  • Use validation diagnostics to recalibrate simulation parameters.

Conclusion

This manuscript will present ISSA-based empirical characterization, derivative-based shape analysis, BVCM estimation, and a BVCM-derived validation framework for simulated human skin reflectance spectra. The central result is a reproducible uncertainty-aware approach for evaluating whether simulated spectra are not only visually plausible, but statistically compatible with real measured skin reflectance.

Draft Abstract Placeholder

Simulated skin reflectance spectra are increasingly used to study skin appearance, imaging systems, and downstream algorithms, but their utility depends on validation against real spectral measurements. We use the International Skin Spectra Archive as an empirical reference dataset and develop a Bayesian varying coefficient model for wavelength-indexed reflectance. We first describe empirical ISSA spectra and introduce derivative-based shape summaries that capture slope and curvature features beyond overall reflectance level. We then estimate wavelength-varying covariate effects and posterior predictive distributions for reflectance spectra. Finally, we use the fitted model to construct a validation framework for simulated spectra, combining pointwise coverage, whole-spectrum discrepancy, and derivative-based shape diagnostics. The framework identifies wavelength regions and shape characteristics where simulations agree with or deviate from empirical skin reflectance, providing an uncertainty-aware tool for improving and validating skin spectrum simulation pipelines.
